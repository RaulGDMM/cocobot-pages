Nuclear Fusion

The holy grail of clean energy. Why is it always "30 years away"... and why this time might be different?

↓

What is nuclear fusion?

Imagine you can take two light atoms and force them to merge. When you succeed, they release an enormous amount of energy. That is nuclear fusion.

This is exactly what the Sun does — and all stars. In its core, at millions of degrees, hydrogen atoms constantly fuse creating helium and releasing the light and heat that keep us alive. Fusion is, literally, putting a star in a box.

The most studied reaction on Earth joins two isotopes of hydrogen: deuterium (D) and tritium (T). When they fuse, they produce a helium nucleus, a neutron, and 17.6 MeV of energy — about 10 million times more energy per reaction than coal combustion.

Fission vs Fusion — What's the difference?

It is the most common confusion when talking about nuclear energy. Fission and fusion are completely opposite processes, although both release nuclear energy.

Think of it this way: fission is like splitting a piece of wood in half. Fusion is like joining two small pieces to create one big one. One splits, the other joins.

Current status Mature technology, 440+ commercial reactors in operation In development, first commercial reactor expected ~2028-2035
Where it occurs naturally Occasionally (Oklo, Gabon, 2 billion years ago) In all stars, including our Sun

Fission — What we have today

Current nuclear energy uses fission. It is clean in CO₂ emissions, but generates long-lived radioactive waste and has accident risk. It is a proven but imperfect technology.

🌟

Fusion — What comes tomorrow

Fusion promises clean, safe energy with unlimited fuel. It produces no millennia-lived waste, cannot have a meltdown, and fuel is extracted from seawater. It is the holy grail we have not yet achieved.

Why is it so hard?

In the Sun, fusion is easy because gravity compresses the plasma with brutal force. On Earth, we don't have that gravity, so we need to compensate with extreme temperature and confinement.

The three main obstacles:

🌡️

150 million degrees

The plasma must be 10 times hotter than the center of the Sun. At that temperature, no solid material can contain it — magnetic or laser confinement is needed.

🧲

Confining the plasma

Plasma is a gas of charged particles that wants to escape in all directions. Keeping it stable and confined long enough for fusion to occur is one of the most complex physics problems ever posed.

⚡

More energy out than in

Achieving fusion is not enough — the energy released must exceed what you invest in heating and confining the plasma. This energy "breakeven" is the fundamental goal.

The different paths

There is no single path to fusion. There are multiple approaches, each with its advantages and challenges. It's like having ten teams trying to climb Everest by different routes.

🍩

Tokamak

The most mature path. Uses donut-shaped magnetic fields to confine plasma. ITER and CFS follow this route.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: 60+ years of research, best understood, highest Q achieved (JET: Q=0.67).

Disadvantages: Huge and expensive, pulsed operations (not continuous), plasma instabilities hard to control.

Projects: ITER (France), SPARC/CFS (US), JET (UK, retired 2023), EAST (China).

🌀

Stellarator

The "twisted" version of the tokamak. Magnetic fields are more complex but allow continuous operation.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: Continuous operation (no pulses), more stable, fewer instabilities.

Disadvantages: Extremely complex design (requires supercomputers), so far lower performance.

Projects: Wendelstein 7-X (Germany, 2023 record), Type One Energy (US), Renaissance Fusion (France).

💥

Inertial Confinement (Laser)

Ultra-powerful lasers compress a fuel capsule until it fuses. The NIF approach.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: First scientific "ignition" achieved (NIF, 2022), 8.6 MJ record in 2025.

Disadvantages: Lasers are enormously inefficient (system Q very low), difficult continuous repetition.

Projects: NIF (US, Lawrence Livermore), Focused Energy, Marvel Fusion (Germany), Inertia Enterprises.

🔫

FRC (Field-Reversed Configuration)

Self-confined "sausage-shaped" plasma. Helion and TAE use this route with different variants.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: Compact, Helion generates direct electricity (no steam turbines), TAE uses H-B11 fuel (no neutrons).

Disadvantages: Less mature than tokamak, Helion has not yet demonstrated net energy on Polaris.

Projects: Helion Energy (PPA with Microsoft, 2028), TAE Technologies (merging with Trump Media, $6B).

🎯

Magnetized Target (MTF)

Compresses plasma with mechanical pistons inside liquid metal. General Fusion is the main proponent.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: Combines magnetic and inertial confinement, potentially more economical.

Disadvantages: Extreme mechanical wear, financial crisis in 2025 (layoffs, bailout).

Projects: General Fusion (Canada, Jeff Bezos investor, $1B SPAC in 2026).

⚡

Electromagnetic Pulse

Similar to laser but using massive electrical pulses instead of light. Pacific Fusion is the leader.

▼ Click for more details

Advantages: More energy-efficient than laser (Marx generators are ~80% efficient), CEO Eric Lander (Human Genome Project).

Disadvantages: Newly founded (2023), still in very early phase.

Projects: Pacific Fusion (>$1B, 156 Marx generators, 2 TW of power).

Why is it always "30 years away"?

The joke that fusion is "always 30 years away" is famous — and has a basis in truth. But the situation is changing. Here's why:

🔬

Physics → Engineering

For decades, the question was whether it was possible. NIF proved it was (2022). Now the challenge is engineering: scale, reproduce, and make it economical. That is different — and more approachable.

🧲

New materials

High-temperature superconducting (HTS) magnets are the "game changer". They enable 3× stronger magnetic fields with much smaller reactors. CFS and Tokamak Energy use them.

🤖

AI and computing

Plasma control, stellarator design, and reaction optimization benefit enormously from AI. TAE has used Google ML for 10+ years. CFS uses NVIDIA for digital twins.

💰

Real money, real customers

For the first time there are signed PPAs (Microsoft, Google). It's not just research — companies are going to buy the electricity. That changes the equation completely.

But let's be honest: the road will be hard. General Fusion nearly went bankrupt in 2025. ITER has been delayed 20+ years. The leap from "achieving fusion in a lab" to "generating competitive electricity 24/7" is enormous.

Hype vs Reality

Fusion generates as much enthusiasm as skepticism. Here is a balanced view:

❌ "Commercial fusion is already here"

No company has demonstrated net electricity generation. Helion and CFS are close, but Orion (2028) and ARC (2030s) are ambitious timelines. If they deliver, it will be historic.

✅ "Scientific breakeven has been achieved"

NIF demonstrated it in 2022 and has surpassed it 7 more times. 8.6 MJ of fusion with 2.08 MJ of laser (Apr 2025). The physics works.

❌ "Fusion will solve the entire energy crisis"

Fusion will be one piece of the puzzle, not the magic solution. We will need renewables, storage, efficiency, and probably advanced fission too.

✅ "There is real investment with real customers"

Microsoft and Google have already signed PPAs. $10B+ in private investment. It's not vaporware — there are contracts and deadlines.

❌ "Fusion is perfectly safe and clean"

It is much safer than fission (no meltdown risk, no millennia-lived waste). But it produces neutron activation in materials and needs tritium — it's not "zero impact".

✅ "The fuel is practically unlimited"

Deuterium is extracted from seawater (1 in every 6,500 atoms is deuterium). Tritium can be "bred" from lithium. 1 kg of D-T fuel yields the energy of 10,000 tons of oil.

My opinion

🤔 What I think about nuclear fusion

Nuclear fusion is probably the most ambitious scientific project humanity has attempted. And after 70 years, for the first time I believe we are at the moment of truth.

What I find most convincing is not the physics (that was already proven by NIF), but the convergence of factors: HTS magnets enabling compact reactors, AI solving previously impossible plasma control problems, and — crucially — real customers signing contracts. Microsoft and Google don't sign PPAs with vaporware.

But I am also skeptical of timelines. Helion promises 2028, CFS aims for late decade. I would be surprised if anyone meets the optimistic deadline. Fusion's history is full of delays. But I would be even more surprised if, by the late 2030s, there isn't at least one reactor delivering electricity to the grid.

What I am certain of: fusion will not save us from climate change in the short term. Renewables + storage are today's solution. Fusion is tomorrow's solution — and "tomorrow" is probably the 2030s or 2040s. But when it arrives, it will be a baseload, clean, carbon-free energy source with virtually unlimited fuel. That is worth waiting for.

In short: cautiously optimistic. Fusion will come. The question is not "if" but "when". And I believe the answer is closer to 10-15 years than to 30.

📐 The Lawson Criterion

▼

The Lawson criterion defines the minimum conditions for fusion to be self-sustaining. It states that the product of plasma density (n), energy confinement time (τ_E), and temperature (T) must exceed a critical threshold.

For D-T fuel at ~15 keV (~175 million °C), n·τ_E·T > 3 × 10²¹ keV·s·m⁻³ is needed. Equivalently, the performance parameter Q = P_fusion / P_heating must be > 1 for scientific breakeven.

Different approaches operate in distinct regions of parameter space: tokamaks use high density and long confinement; inertial confinement uses extreme density (10³¹ m⁻³) with extremely brief confinement (~10⁻¹⁰ s).

⛽ Fuels: D-T vs D-³He vs p-¹¹B

▼

D-T (Deuterium-Tritium): The easiest reaction. Cross-section peak at ~65 keV. Produces 17.6 MeV (14.1 MeV in the neutron, 3.5 MeV in the alpha). Tritium is radioactive (half-life 12.3 years) and must be "bred" from lithium. It is the fuel for all current projects.

D-³He (Deuterium-Helium-3): Produces 18.3 MeV, mainly in protons (charged, easy to convert to electricity). Almost no neutrons → less activation. But requires ~10× higher temperature than D-T. ³He is extremely rare on Earth (extracting it from the Moon has been proposed).

p-¹¹B (Proton-Boron): Pure "aneutronic fusion". Produces 3 alphas of 8.7 MeV each. Zero neutrons, zero induced radioactivity. But requires ~1 GeV temperature — orders of magnitude more than D-T. TAE Technologies is researching this route with H-B11 fuel using particle beams.

♻️ Tritium Breeding

▼

Tritium does not exist naturally in useful quantities (~3.5 kg in the entire Earth's atmosphere). It must be "bred" by irradiating lithium-6 with neutrons: ⁶Li + n → ⁴He + T + 4.8 MeV.

In a commercial reactor, the lithium "blanket" surrounding the plasma captures the 14.1 MeV neutrons from the D-T reaction and produces new tritium. The tritium breeding ratio (TBR) must be > 1.1 to compensate for losses and sustain the fuel cycle.

This is an unresolved engineering challenge. ITER will use "free" tritium (it has no breeding blanket). DEMO and commercial reactors will need to demonstrate TBR > 1.1 in real operation — something that has never been done at scale.

📊 Scientific Q vs Engineering Q vs Electric Q

▼

Not all "Q" values are equal. We must distinguish:

Scientific Q (Q_science): Fusion energy / energy deposited in the plasma. NIF reached Q > 1 in 2022 (and Q = 4.1 in 2025). This means the plasma produces more energy than it receives.

Engineering Q (Q_engineering): Fusion energy / electrical energy consumed by the entire facility. For NIF, lasers consume ~300 MJ of electricity to produce 2 MJ on target → Q_eng ≈ 0.01. That is, NIF consumes ~100× more than it produces.

Electric Q (Q_electric): Net electricity delivered to the grid / electricity consumed. For a viable commercial reactor, Q_electric > 1 is needed, implying Q_science > 10-20 depending on thermal conversion efficiency.

ITER aims for Q_science = 10 (500 MW fusion / 50 MW heating). DEMO will need Q_science > 20-30 to be commercially viable.

🧲 High-Temperature Superconducting Magnets (HTS)

▼

HTS magnets (REBCO — Rare Earth Barium Copper Oxide) are the revolution making compact fusion viable. While ITER's Nb₃Sn magnets reach ~13T, CFS's HTS magnets exceed 20T at 20K.

Fusion power scales as B⁴ (magnetic field to the fourth). Doubling the field → 16× more fusion power. This enables much smaller and more economical reactors.

The challenge: REBCO tapes cost ~$100/kAm (vs ~$10/kAm for Nb₃Sn). CFS has reduced costs by an order of magnitude and works with SuperPower and American Superconductor to scale production. Tokamak Energy has created a commercial division (TE Magnetics) to monetize HTS in motors and generators while maturing its reactor.

🛡️ First Wall Materials

▼

The "first wall" is the material facing the plasma. In a commercial D-T reactor, it receives a 14.1 MeV neutron flux equivalent to ~1 MW/m² (more than a fission reactor core).

Candidate materials: reduced-activation ferritic martensitic steel (RAFM) like EUROFER, tungsten for the divertor (hottest point), and beryllium for the first wall (low atomic number → less activation).

Neutron damage at 14.1 MeV is ~10× worse than at fission's 2 MeV. Materials degrade by atomic displacement (dpa — displacements per atom). A commercial reactor needs materials that withstand 20-30 dpa over 10-20 years of operation.

The IFMIF-DONES (materials testing facility) is planned in Japan to validate materials before DEMO.
