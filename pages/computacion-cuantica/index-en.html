Quantum Computing

The future is not computed. It is superposed.

Fundamentals

What is quantum computing?

The computers you use now run on bits: tiny switches that are either on (1) or off (0). Everything your computer does — from watching a video to editing a document — is trillions of these switches changing state.

Quantum computing uses qubits (quantum bits). And this is where things get strange.

💡 Coin Analogy

Imagine a coin on the table. It is either heads or tails. That is a classical bit: a defined state. Now flip the coin in the air while it spins. While spinning, it is heads AND tails at the same time. That is a qubit in superposition. Only when you catch it and look at it ("measure" it) does it collapse to a definite state.

🏁 Maze Analogy

A classical computer solving a maze tries one path, hits a dead end, goes back and tries another. A quantum computer, thanks to superposition, can explore all paths of the maze simultaneously. It is not magic: it is physics.

👆 Click to "measure" the qubit

Each click is a "measurement". The result is random because the qubit was in superposition.

Quantum Mechanics

The Three Quantum Superpowers

Quantum computing exploits three phenomena of quantum mechanics that have no equivalent in our everyday world. They are counterintuitive, but they have been experimentally demonstrated again and again.

🌀
Superposition
A qubit can exist in a state that is a combination of 0 and 1 simultaneously. It is not "sometimes 0, sometimes 1" — it is both at once, like a wave occupying multiple points at the same time. When measured, it collapses to a single value.
🔗
Entanglement
Two qubits can become "entangled": the state of one is instantaneously correlated with the other, regardless of distance. Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance". Measuring one instantly determines the result of the other.
🌊
Interference
Like water waves, quantum states can interfere with each other. "Correct" possibilities reinforce each other (constructive interference) and incorrect ones cancel out (destructive interference). This is how a quantum computer can "find" the correct answer among millions of possibilities.

The Great Challenge

Why is it so hard? — Quantum Error Correction

Here is the big problem that holds back all of quantum computing: qubits are incredibly fragile.

In a classical computer, bits are robust. A transistor on or off does not change by itself. But a qubit is perturbed by anything: a temperature fluctuation, cosmic radiation, mechanical vibrations, ambient magnetic fields... Any interaction with the environment destroys its quantum state. This is called decoherence.

💡 Analogy

Imagine trying to build a sandcastle on the beach while the wind blows, it rains, and the tide rises. Every second, something threatens to destroy it. That is keeping a qubit in a quantum state: a race against the noise of the entire universe.

So why not just copy the qubit as a backup?

Because quantum mechanics forbids copying an unknown quantum state. It is called the no-cloning theorem. You also cannot "look" at the qubit to check for errors, because looking at it (measuring it) destroys it. It is like trying to check if an egg is broken... by breaking it.

The solution: Quantum Error Correction (QEC)

The clever solution is to encode a single "logical qubit" distributed across many "physical qubits". Instead of measuring individual qubits (which would destroy them), the correlations between them are measured. This allows errors to be detected and corrected without ever revealing the protected quantum state.

💡 Analogy

Imagine you want to send a fragile message along a bumpy road. Instead of sending it once, you write it on 100 different letters using a special code. If some letters arrive damaged, the code lets you reconstruct the original message without any individual letter needing to be perfect. That is, in essence, quantum error correction.

The cost: the "overhead"

The problem is the cost. The most popular approach, the surface code, needs approximately 1,000 physical qubits to create a single reliable logical qubit. To do something truly useful — like breaking RSA-2048 — an estimated 20 to 4,000 logical qubits are needed. That means millions of physical qubits.

Today the best machines have thousands of physical qubits, but none are "logical" (reliable). We are in the foundation-building phase.

Recent milestones

  • ✅ Google (2024): First demonstration of QEC below the "threshold" — a distance-7 code with 101 qubits, error rate of only 0.143% per cycle. For the first time, adding more qubits reduces errors.
  • ✅ Quantinuum (2025): Fault-tolerant non-Clifford gate with only 8 physical qubits using trapped ions.
  • ✅ Microsoft Majorana: Topological protection in hardware → ~10x less overhead than previous approaches. If it works at scale, it could change everything.
⚡ In summary

Without error correction, quantum computers are laboratory curiosities that make too many errors to be useful. With error correction, they become real tools. It is the great bottleneck that everyone is trying to solve.

What is Microsoft's Majorana chip?

In February 2025, Microsoft announced Majorana 1, the world's first quantum processor based on topological qubits. It is a radically different approach from IBM or Google.

Instead of using conventional superconductors, Microsoft combined indium arsenide (semiconductor) with aluminum (superconductor) to create a new state of matter: the topoconductor. At the ends of these nanowires appear the so-called Majorana Zero Modes (MZMs): quasiparticles that store quantum information in a "non-local" way.

Why does it matter? Because the information is topologically protected from environmental noise. It is as if the material itself "wraps" the information in a bubble that noise cannot penetrate. The result: ~10 times fewer physical qubits needed for error correction than previous approaches.

The roadmap: start with 8-qubit arrays, scale to 27×13, and finally reach 1 million qubits on a single chip. Microsoft is working with DARPA on the US2QC program to build a fault-tolerant prototype in "years, not decades".

The Convergence

AI + Quantum: The fusion of the future

Two of the most transformative technologies of our time are converging. Quantum computing and artificial intelligence not only benefit each other — together they could unlock capabilities that today seem like science fiction.

Why does AI need quantum?

⚡
Exponentially faster training
Current AI models require weeks or months of training on thousands of GPUs. Quantum algorithms could speed up key operations like gradient optimization and the processing of massive search spaces.
🧠
Impossible search spaces
Quantum superposition allows exploring huge regions of the solution space simultaneously. For problems like protein design or molecule discovery, this is the difference between "impossible" and "minutes".
🔋
Energy efficiency
Training large AI models consumes massive amounts of energy. D-Wave highlights how quantum computing could address the growing energy consumption of AI — quantum annealers are notably energy-efficient.

Quantum Machine Learning (QML)

QML combines neural networks with quantum circuits. The most promising algorithms include:

  • VQE (Variational Quantum Eigensolver): Simulates molecules and materials — already used to discover new catalysts and drugs.
  • QAOA (Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm): Combinatorial optimization — clustering, feature selection, portfolio optimization.
  • Quantum Neural Networks: Neural networks where the "layers" are parametric quantum circuits.

Key data

Google (oct 2025) 13,000× speedup over the Frontier supercomputer in physical simulations using only 65 qubits
D-Wave Quantum-AI hybrid solvers in production: ML optimization, feature selection, clustering for real clients
Microsoft Azure Quantum integrates QML into its cloud platform, with a roadmap toward fault-tolerant for large-scale chemical simulation
Industria (2026) The Convergence quantum-AI convergence is considered "the new era of computing" — more inversiones que nunca
⚠️ Reality check

Although the potential is enormous, most applications today are hybrid: part of the work is done by a classical computer, another part by a quantum one. Real "quantum advantage" for AI — where a quantum computer clearly outperforms any classical GPU — is probably 5-10 years away. But the trend is clear and investment is massive.

Cybersecurity

The end of digital security?

One of the most worrying (and most exaggerated) topics is the impact of quantum computing on cryptography. Let us put it in perspective.

Shor's algorithm

In 1994, mathematician Peter Shor proved that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer could factor large numbers exponentially faster than any classical computer. Why does it matter? Because RSA, ECC and Diffie-Hellman — the protocols that protect the internet, online banking, military communications and everything encrypted — rely on large number factorization being "impossible" for current computers.

With a fault-tolerant quantum computer with enough qubits, Shor's algorithm would break these encryptions in hours or minutes. This is mathematically proven. It is not speculation.

🛡️ Verdict

It is not "they hack everything tomorrow". It is a race with a deadline, and it is already running. The threat is real and mathematically proven, but it is years away — probably a decade. The good news: post-quantum cryptography is already standardized and migration has begun. Those who migrate early will be protected. Those who wait until the last moment, will not.

Perspective

Hype vs Reality

Quantum computing generates more sensationalist headlines than almost any other technology. Let us separate the wheat from the chaff.

❌ What they say

"Quantum computing will hack everything in the next 2 years."

✅ What is real

The most credible estimate for breaking RSA-2048 is the mid-2030s. Moreover, migration to post-quantum cryptography is already underway.

❌ What they say

"Quantum computers will replace our current computers."

✅ What is real

Quantum only accelerates specific types of problems (optimization, quantum simulation, factorization). For browsing, watching videos or writing documents, your classical computer is better.

❌ What they say

"We already have quantum computers that solve everything."

✅ What is real

Commercial quantum computers do exist (D-Wave, IBM, etc.), but they are specialized, noisy, and have not yet reached "quantum advantage" for most problems.

❌ What they say

"Quantum AI will solve everything tomorrow."

✅ What is real

Most current applications are hybrid (classical + quantum). Real quantum advantage for AI is probably 5-10 years away. The potential is enormous, but timelines are realistic.

❌ What they say

"We are just around the corner from useful quantum computing."

✅ What is real

There is genuine and accelerating progress, but experts estimate 5-10 years for industrial-scale fault-tolerant. It is a marathon, not a sprint.

Go Deeper

Want more technical details?

If your curiosity has been piqued and you want to understand the "how" behind the "what", click on any of these sections to go deeper.

A gate-model quantum computer (like those from IBM or Google) works with quantum circuits: sequences of operations (gates) applied to qubits.

Fundamental quantum gates:

  • Hadamard (H): Creates superposition. Apply H to a |0⟩ qubit and you get (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 — a combination of both states.
  • CNOT: Entangles two qubits. If the control qubit is |1⟩, it applies NOT to the target qubit. It is the gate that creates quantum correlations.
  • Pauli-X/Y/Z: Qubit rotations on the Bloch sphere (the geometric model of a qubit).
  • Phase (S, T): Introduce phase shifts that enable constructive/destructive interference.

The Bloch sphere is the geometric representation of a qubit: a point on the surface of a sphere. The north pole is |0⟩, the south pole is |1⟩, and any intermediate point is a superposition. Quantum gates rotate this point.

Shor's algorithm (the one that breaks RSA) combines:

  • A quantum Fourier transform (QFT) to find periodicities
  • Superposition to evaluate all possibilities simultaneously
  • Interference para amplificar la respuesta correcta

The trick: finding the period of a function is equivalent to factoring a number. A classical computer takes exponential time. Shor does it in polynomial time.

Not all quantum computers are the same. Each approach has advantages and disadvantages:

  • Superconductors (IBM, Google, Rigetti): Qubits made of superconducting electrical circuits cooled to ~15 mK (near absolute zero). Advantages: manufacturable with semiconductor technology. Disadvantages: very sensitive to noise, need for extreme cryogenics.
  • Quantum Annealing (D-Wave): Does not use gates, but a quantum "annealing" process to find the energy minimum of an optimization problem. Advantages: already commercial, thousands of qubits. Disadvantages: only useful for optimization, not universal.
  • Trapped ions (IonQ, Quantinuum): Qubits formed by individual ions suspended in electromagnetic fields. Advantages: high fidelity, long coherence time. Disadvantages: hard to scale, slow.
  • Photons (Xanadu, PsiQuantum): Light-based qubits. Advantages: operate at room temperature, compatible with optical fiber. Disadvantages: hard to create deterministic gates.
  • Silicon spins (Intel): Qubits based on the spin of individual electrons in silicon chips. Advantages: compatible with CMOS manufacturing. Disadvantages: very early stage.
  • Topological (Microsoft Majorana): Qubits topologically protected by Majorana Zero Modes. Advantages: inherent error protection (~10x less overhead). Disadvantages: extremely difficult to manufacture, still in demonstration phase.

QML seeks to exploit quantum advantage for machine learning tasks:

  • VQE (Variational Quantum Eigensolver): Combines a quantum circuit (which prepares quantum states) with a classical optimizer. Used to find the ground state energy of molecules — key in drug and materials discovery.
  • QAOA (Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm): Designed for combinatorial optimization problems. Alternates between a "problem Hamiltonian" and a "mixing Hamiltonian" to converge toward the optimal solution.
  • Quantum Neural Networks (QNN): Networks where the "layers" are parametric quantum circuits. Parameters are adjusted with a classical optimizer (similar to backpropagation).
  • HQS (Hybrid Quantum-Classical): The current dominant model. Part of the pipeline is classical, part quantum. Example: a classical computer prepares data, a quantum one performs a specific operation (like a quantum kernel), and the classical one processes the result.

Current State (2026): Most demonstrations are "proof of concept" on NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) devices. True advantage is expected with fault-tolerant.

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) are encryption algorithms designed to be resistant to both classical and quantum computers:

  • CRYSTALS-Kyber: NIST standard for public-key encryption (Key Encapsulation Mechanism). Lattice-based. Fast and efficient.
  • CRYSTALS-Dilithium: NIST standard for digital signatures. Also lattice-based.
  • SPHINCS+: Hash-based digital signature. Slower but with security based on hash primitives (very well studied).
  • FALCON: Digital signature with more compact signatures. Lattice-based with special structure.

Why lattices? "Learning With Errors" (LWE) and related lattice problems are considered resistant to Shor's algorithm because they are not based on factorization or discrete logarithms.

Migration is already underway: TLS 1.3 with PQC, OpenSSL updates, and major browsers and operating systems are integrating these algorithms.

Infrastructure

🇨🇳 China's quantum networks: Unhackable Internet

While the world debates whether quantum computing will break cryptography, China is already building communication networks that would be impossible to hack, even with a quantum computer. And they are doing it on an unprecedented scale.

How does an "unhackable" network work?

It is called QKD (Quantum Key Distribution). Instead of using complex mathematics to encrypt, it uses the laws of quantum physics. The idea is simple but brilliant:

💡 Analogy

Imagine you send a letter to your friend using a special envelope. If someone tries to open it along the way, the envelope self-destructs instantly. It is not that the message is encrypted — it is that any eavesdropping attempt is physically detectable. That is QKD: if a spy tries to intercept the quantum keys, the entanglement breaks and both sides know instantly.

The key is sent using entangled photons through optical fiber. If someone tries to measure or copy those photons, Heisenberg's uncertainty principle guarantees that the quantum state changes — and that change is detectable. This is not theory: it is fundamental physics.

China is leading the way

China is not playing around. It has invested billions in building the most ambitious quantum infrastructure in the world:

Milestone Detail
2016: Micius World's first quantum satellite. Demonstrated satellite QKD between China and Austria (7,000 km). Cost: ~$100M.
2017: Beijing-Shanghai Quantum optical fiber network of 2,000 km connecting Beijing, Shanghai and intermediate cities. The longest QKD network in the world. In use for government and banking communications.
2025: Jinan-1 World's first quantum microsatellite. Real-time QKD with multiple ground stations in China and South Africa. Intercontinental: 12,900 km.
2025: CN-QCN Carrier-grade network of 10,000+ km with 145 nodes. The most extensive quantum network on the planet.
2025: China-Rusia Secure China-Russia communications using Micius and ground station network.
Feb 2026: Quantum repeater First scalable quantum repeater block: secure key distribution over 10 km with long-duration quantum memory. Breaks the distance barrier of fiber networks.
2026: QKD 343 km Entanglement-based QKD over 343 km of optical fiber with multi-wavelength polarization compensation. Published in Science China.

Why is this so difficult?

The fundamental problem: photons are lost in optical fiber. At ~100 km, most have disappeared. In a classical computer, you simply amplify the signal. In the quantum world, you cannot copy or amplify a quantum state (no-cloning theorem).

The solution: quantum repeaters. Unlike classical repeaters that copy the signal, a quantum repeater uses quantum teleportation: it entangles pairs of photons in short segments and then "connects" those segments to extend entanglement over long distances. It is like passing a message from person to person instead of shouting it across a field.

China demonstrated the first scalable quantum repeater block in February 2026. It is the key missing component for building continental quantum networks.

🌍 In context

China is not only leading in quantum computing — it is building the communication infrastructure of the future. A quantum optical fiber network spanning 10,000+ km with 145 nodes is not a laboratory experiment: it is a real, production network protecting government and financial communications. While the West debates, China builds.

Analysis

My opinion

Quantum computing is one of those technologies that generate more noise than signal. Every week there is a headline saying "quantum has achieved X" and the next day another saying "quantum is a fraud". Reality, as almost always, is in the middle.

What is real: there is genuine and accelerating progress. D-Wave sells quantum computers that solve real optimization problems. Google demonstrated 13,000× speedups in physical simulations. Microsoft, after 20 years of research, presented the world's first topological chip. Qubits are more stable than ever, and error correction — the great bottleneck — is finally showing promising results.

What is hype: the idea that "in two years there will be no security on the internet" is alarmism. The most credible estimate points to the mid-2030s, and migration to post-quantum cryptography is already underway. Anyone who says "quantum will replace your laptop" does not understand what it is about.

What excites me most: the AI + quantum fusion. Not because it will happen tomorrow, but because the medium-term potential is transformative. Imagine an AI system that can simulate millions of molecules simultaneously to discover new drugs, materials or catalysts. Or a language model trained with quantum acceleration that can process knowledge spaces impossible for current GPUs. It is the kind of leap that goes beyond "faster" — it is "something that was previously impossible".

And Microsoft's Majorana chip deserves special mention. If topological protection scales as they promise, it could solve the error correction problem at its root, not with software patches but with fundamental physics. It would be like going from building a wall brick by brick to pouring a monolithic concrete block. If it works, it changes the rules of the game. If not, it still teaches us a lot about the physics of condensed matter.

In summary: it is not the holy grail that will solve everything tomorrow. But it is not smoke either. It is a technology under construction, with 5-10 year timelines for real impact, that deserves attention without fanaticism. The "early bet" moment has already begun.
