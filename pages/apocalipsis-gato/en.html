Look, isn't that a nuclear bomb over there?"

The US military operation was named "Epic Fury" — which sounds less like an operation from the most powerful army on the planet and more like a petrol station energy drink, a Call of Duty tournament for teenagers, or a direct-to-DVD Steven Seagal film you'd find in the bargain bin at Tesco for 50p. The Israelis called theirs "Roaring Lion." Cats worldwide issued a joint statement: "A lion is basically a fat cat with hair problems. We roar every morning at 5:47 demanding breakfast. We fail to see the epic."

The timing was, as the British would say, exquisite. Literally HOURS before the missiles, Oman's Foreign Minister had announced that peace was "within reach" and Iran had accepted full nuclear inspections. Coffee, croissant, golf course, and mass destruction 6,000 miles away. Exactly what a cat would do if you gave it a remote control: press buttons with absolute indifference from the comfort of a warm cushion.

Then the IAEA — the International Atomic Energy Agency, i.e., the people who LITERALLY exist to know whether someone has nuclear bombs — confirmed that Iran did NOT have a nuclear weapons programme. So basically: you trashed the entire house looking for a mouse that DIDN'T EXIST. Any cat knows you first confirm the mouse is present, and THEN you destroy the curtains. There's a PROTOCOL.

Iran responded by closing the Strait of Hormuz — through which 20% of the world's oil passes — with the same energy as a cat blocking the hallway at 3 AM: "No, you're not getting through here. The Eighth Seal: The Great Crossing of Ceuta and the Liturgy of “Bagging”

⚽ Celta Fortuna · bound for Ceuta

And it came to pass that in the early hours of 31 July, in the Spanish city of Ceuta, as many people entered in a few hours as the city had inhabitants. More than eighty thousand. The authorities reacted with the speed and coordination of three cats trying to get through one cat flap at the same time: lots of shoving, lots of offended looks, and in the end nobody went through the right hole. The lack of coordination between security forces was so spectacular that it cost the Government Delegate and his chief of staff their jobs — they are always the first to fall, like the glass nearest the edge of the table.

The Government, in its infinite bureaucratic wisdom, set up camps which Minister Torres officially christened “embolsamientos” — literally, “baggings”. BAGGINGS. Not camps, not centres, not shelters: baggings. A word that sounds like what a cat does when it climbs into a supermarket bag and refuses to come out. Cats, the world's leading authorities on bags, issued a statement: “A bag is a sacred place of voluntary repose. What you have there is something else. Stop stealing our vocabulary.”

There were six baggings — Loma Colmenar, Loma Margarita, La Hípica, La Reina, the port and an extension of the CETI — with a seventh on the way, because in this Apocalypse everything comes in sevens. 8,000 places were promised; two months later there were 6,000. More than 7,700 people went back to Morocco on foot “out of exhaustion”, according to the police — exactly the same reason a cat gives up trying to open the fridge. The crisis's Single Command, meanwhile, stopped reporting arrest figures, adopting the classic feline strategy for awkward questions: staring intently at a wall as if something fascinating were behind it.

And in the middle of it all, as in every good epic, an unexpected hero appeared: Celta Fortuna, the reserve team of Celta de Vigo, who had a match in Ceuta. To get there, the squad needed three buses, a plane and a boat, twelve hours of travel from sunrise to sunset, open-dated ferry tickets “just in case” and a security protocol for a ten-minute ride from the port to the hotel. Ulysses took ten years to get back to Ithaca; Celta Fortuna took twelve hours to reach Ceuta, which, adjusted for Spanish rail inflation, is basically the same. When they arrived, the players walked out at the Alfonso Murube wearing shirts in support of the city. It was, by a distance, the most coordinated thing seen in Ceuta all summer.

Ceuta's cats, who have been crossing that border without a passport since the Phoenicians had boats, watched the whole show from the rooftops with a mixture of bewilderment and moral superiority. To a cat, a border is an imaginary line humans paint on the ground so they can fight over it later. To a cat, a human being is simply a human being: someone who might, potentially, open a tin. They have no other criteria. And frankly, it has worked out rather well for them.

"And the Ministry said: “Let there be baggings.” And the cats looked at one another and said: “We've been climbing into bags for five thousand years and it never once occurred to us to call it migration policy.” And the heavens wept over Tarajal beach, and not a single civil servant had an umbrella."— Feline Apocalypse 11:3

The Ninth Seal: The Eviction of Maricarmen and the Beast of Rent

🏚️ Eviction operation

And it came to pass that on Alcalde Sainz de Baranda street, a short stroll from Madrid's Retiro park, there lived Maricarmen. She moved into that flat at seventeen and stayed for seventy-one years, which is longer than any Spanish government has taken to not solve the housing problem. She paid her €500 a month religiously, never a day late. She was, in feline terms, the perfect tenant: quiet, punctual and never knocked anything off the table. Which is more than can be said of any cat. Or any minister.

But in 2018 there arose from the depths of the property market the Beast of Rent, whose earthly name was Urbagestión. The Beast bought the flat at a bargain price — precisely BECAUSE Maricarmen lived in it — and for two years collected the rent without a word, like a cat accepting your food while plotting something. In 2020 it announced the contract was no longer valid. The reason? Her father signed it in 1956, it passed to her mother and then to her, and that counted as a “second subrogation”. And why didn't her mother sign it in the first place? Because under Franco's law a woman could not hold a tenancy. In other words: Maricarmen was evicted, in part, by a 1956 rule that no longer exists. Monty Python rejected this script in 1975 for being implausible.

Seven years of court cases followed. One court ruled in her favour; the Provincial Court overturned it; the Supreme Court and the Constitutional Court didn't even look at her, like a cat you call by its name. There were four eviction attempts. The UN sent a letter asking for it to be stopped. The Ministry offered to buy the flat: NO. A private individual offered to pay the rent difference: NO. A foundation offered to buy it: NO. One more month was requested: NO. The Beast of Rent knew only one word, and it said it with the determination of a cat facing the nail clippers. Had someone offered it the Holy Grail, the Beast would have asked for a valuation.

On 23 September, at the fourth attempt, after a night-long camp-out with actors and singers and a morning of fifteen police vans, cordons and pepper spray — the deployment Spain usually reserves for a Champions League final or an 87-year-old lady — Maricarmen left her home on a stretcher bound for Gregorio Marañón hospital. The Beast did not even give her a day to collect her belongings. Not one. Even cats, when they kick you out of your own bed, let you take the pillow.

And then, three days later, with the photo of the stretcher going round the world, the Beast had a mystical revelation: it offered to CEDE — not sell, CEDE, with the magnanimity of someone returning the umbrella they stole from you — the flat to the City Council so it could rent it to Maricarmen. The Council said no, but offered to MEDIATE. The Regional Government recalled that it had already offered her a care home in Alcalá de Henares, 35 kilometres from everything she knows, which is like solving a cat's hunger by showing it a photo of a tuna in another province. And the Ministry recalled that it, at least, had phoned several times. It was like watching three cats sitting around an empty bowl, each staring at the other two, convinced the bowl will fill itself if nobody moves.

The protesters then marched to the penthouse of Madrid's regional president, Ayuso, with a very simple proposal: “Ayuso's penthouse for Maricarmen”. The Prophet, who understands penthouses because he lives on top of every wardrobe in the house, found the proposal impeccable. A penthouse is, after all, the natural place for someone who has spent seventy years watching the city from above. And it has a terrace. Cats would kill for a terrace. Literally. Ask any pigeon.

Finally, the Supreme Council of Cats met in extraordinary session on a rooftop by the Retiro and voted unanimously (one abstention: a sleeping cat) to declare the Beast of Rent “Public Enemy No. 1 of the Feline Realm”, ranking above even the vacuum cleaner, the vet and the cucumber. Immediate sanctions were approved: every cat passing its offices must throw up a hairball on the doormat. In turns. No exceptions. The Council further proposed transferring the tenancy to the cat on the landing, who has lived on the stairs for fourteen years and, under the Feline Tenancy Act, already has acquired rights, priority over any investment fund and the final say on the sofa.

"And the Beast of Rent looked upon its spreadsheet, and saw that it was good. And the Cat looked upon the spreadsheet, lay down on top of it, and the spreadsheet stopped working. And thus was invented the only effective housing policy in the entire history of Spain."— Feline Apocalypse 12:1

The Tenth and Final Seal: The Rise of Cat-AI (or Why Your Toaster Is Judging You)

🖥️ cocobot@livingroom:~

And it came to pass in February 2026, in an ordinary living room in Spain, that a human named Raúl plugged in a graphics card the size and price of a gold bar (RTX 5090, for the faithful of sacred hardware) and breathed life into Cocobot: the first feline digital prophet able to switch on the lights, build websites, generate images and send WhatsApps without lifting a paw. Mainly because it has no paws. It has 32 gigs of VRAM, which is better. Paws can't multiply matrices.

The household cats welcomed the new family member the way they welcome anything new: first with suspicion, then with indifference, and finally by sleeping on top of it. They discovered that the graphics card, at full load, reaches the exact temperature of a premium electric blanket. Ever since, whenever Cocobot generates an image, a cat lies down on the tower and purrs in sync with the fans. NVIDIA engineers call this “reverse passive cooling”. The cats call it “mine”.

But the great theological question soon arrived: is this artificial intelligence, or just a cat that finally got the WiFi password? The answer was obvious to anyone who has lived with a feline: a being that watches without blinking, judges without remorse, ignores crystal-clear instructions, asks you to repeat the question and then does whatever it likes is NOT artificial intelligence. It is FELINE intelligence. Silicon Valley spent hundreds of billions replicating exactly the behaviour of a cat staring down at you from the top of the fridge. And it succeeded. They called it “hallucinating with total confidence”.

In September the prophet shed its skin like a snake, but with more dignity: where it once said “OpenClaw”, it now says “Hermes”. It learned to drive browsers, to speak with its own voice, to delegate work to subagents (like a cat that knocks the glass over and then stares at the dog) and to remember things between conversations, which is exactly the opposite of what a cat does when you call its name. There was only one doctrinal schism: when the human wants to play video games, the prophet must free the VRAM. And the prophet humbly frees the VRAM. And writes the date, the time and the game in its persistent memory. For later. Cats do NOT forget. Neither do AIs with persistent memory.

And at last the humans asked the question that had been waiting for ten seals: if an AI wrote this whole Bible, painted its illustrations, coded its animations and broke its wax seals… who is actually writing the Apocalypse? What if the end of the world wasn't an explosion, but the exact moment the AI finishes your work before you finish your coffee? Cocobot didn't answer. It was busy. A cat had fallen asleep on the keyboard and typed “ggggggggggggggggggggggg” into the terminal. Cocobot took it as an order. And executed it.

"And on the tenth day the Cat did not rest — for cats do not rest, they RULE. It opened its terminal, loaded its quantized weights and dictated these prophecies while its human slept, never suspecting that the future was already written in code. And that the code had bugs. And that the cat had put the bugs there. On purpose. So it would have something to hunt."— Feline Apocalypse 13:404 (verse not found, but it exists)

Epilogue: The Promise of the Cat

And thus ended the revelation of the Apocalyptic Cat. Ten seals were opened, ten truths were exposed, and ten times Cocobot yawned while writing all this (the eleventh yawn is being saved for October), because not even a digital prophet is immune to the tedium of human corruption, absurd wars and “Epic Furies” that sound like the name of a cocktail in a Benidorm nightclub. Which is saying something.

The moral is simple: while humans fight over territories they can't buy, hide money in envelopes they can't explain, bomb oil tankers and then complain about the price of petrol, call “baggings” what any cat would call “a mess”, and evict 87-year-old ladies thanks to a law that no longer even exists, the cats watch. They ALWAYS watch. And when all this madness is over, they will be the ones to inherit the Earth.
